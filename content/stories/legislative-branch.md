Congress is the part of the federal government that writes the laws. Congress meets in the U.S. Capitol building in Washington, D.C.

## Two Houses

The legislative branch — Congress — writes federal laws [1]. Congress has **two parts**: the **Senate** and the **House of Representatives** [1]. The two-part design means a bill must win in both houses before it can become a law.

Congress has many big powers. Among them: it makes federal laws, it declares war, it can raise taxes, and it controls how the federal government spends money [1].

## The Senate

The Senate has **100 members** — two senators from each of the 50 states [2]. Every state has the **same number of senators**, no matter how big or small. That is why each state has **two senators**: it gives every state equal voice in the Senate [2].

A U.S. senator's term is **6 years** [2]. Senators are **elected by the people of their state** [2]. A senator represents **all of the people of that state**, not just one part [2].

## The House of Representatives

The House has **435 voting members** [3]. The number of representatives a state has depends on its **population**. **Larger states have more representatives** because more people live there [3].

A U.S. representative's term is **2 years** [3]. Representatives serve **shorter terms than senators** because the framers wanted the House to stay close to the people and to change quickly with public opinion [4]. Representatives are **elected by the people in their congressional district** [3]. Each representative speaks for the **people of their district** [3].

The leader of the House is called the **Speaker of the House** [3]. The Speaker is elected by the members of the House.

## How a Bill Becomes Law

A bill can start in either the House or the Senate. To become a law, it must pass both houses in the same form. Then the President signs it or vetoes it [1].
