The executive branch carries out the laws. Its leader is the President. The President works in the White House.

## The President

The **President** is in charge of the executive branch [1]. The President serves a term of **4 years** [1]. To stay in office past the first term, the President must win a second election. The 22nd Amendment limits the President to two terms.

The President has many big jobs. The President signs bills into law. The President can also **veto** bills — that is, refuse to sign them [1]. The President is the **Commander in Chief** of the U.S. military [1]. The President leads U.S. talks with other countries. The President also **picks federal judges**, including Supreme Court justices [1].

## Succession and the Vice President

If the President can no longer serve, the **Vice President becomes President** [2]. If the Vice President can no longer serve, the **Speaker of the House becomes President** next in line [2].

The Vice President's main jobs are to take over if needed and to lead the Senate. There the VP can break tie votes [2].

## The Cabinet

The President has help. A group of top advisers called the **Cabinet** advises the President on every big area of government [3]. Cabinet members lead the executive departments. Two well-known Cabinet jobs are the **Secretary of State** and the **Secretary of Defense** [3]. Other Cabinet jobs include the Secretary of the Treasury, the Secretary of Education, and the Attorney General [3].

The executive branch has many other parts too. The **Vice President** serves in it. So does the **Cabinet**. So do the federal departments and agencies — like the State Department, the Defense Department, and the Department of Justice [3].

## How the President Is Picked

The United States does not pick the President by a direct national vote. Voters in each state pick **electors**. Those electors then cast the official votes for President. This system is called the **Electoral College** [4]. It is important because it spreads the choice of President across the states. Smaller states still get a real say.
