American citizens have rights that the government cannot take away. Citizens also have duties they should carry out. Together these make a working democracy.

## Rights for Everyone in the United States

Some rights belong to **everyone living in the United States**, not just to citizens [1]. The Bill of Rights names these rights. Three of them are [1]:

- Freedom of speech.
- Freedom of religion.
- Freedom of assembly (gathering in public).

The First Amendment also protects freedom of the press and the right to ask the government to fix problems [1]. These rights are protected even for people who are not yet citizens [1].

## Voting Rights — Many Amendments

Voting is one of the great rights of citizens. **Four amendments** to the Constitution changed who can vote in federal elections [2]:

- The **15th Amendment** says a citizen cannot be denied the vote because of race or color.
- The **19th Amendment** gives women the right to vote.
- The **24th Amendment** ends the poll tax (a fee for voting).
- The **26th Amendment** sets the voting age at **18**.

To **vote in a federal election, run for federal office, or serve on a jury**, a person must be a **U.S. citizen who is at least 18 years old** [3].

## How People Become U.S. Citizens

People become U.S. citizens in a few ways [4]:

- **Born in the United States.** Anyone born on U.S. soil is a citizen at birth.
- **Born to a U.S. parent.** A child born abroad to a U.S. citizen parent can be a citizen at birth.
- **Naturalize.** A non-citizen lawful permanent resident can become a citizen by going through naturalization. The civics test you are studying for is part of this path.
- **Derive citizenship.** A child can sometimes become a citizen automatically when a parent naturalizes.

## Promises Citizens Make

The **Pledge of Allegiance** shows loyalty to the **U.S. flag and to the United States** [3]. New citizens take a longer promise called the **Oath of Allegiance**. They give up loyalty to other countries, swear to defend the **Constitution and the laws** of the United States, swear to obey the laws, and promise to serve the country (in the military or in another way) if needed [3].

## Civic Participation and Service

There are many ways to take part in American civic life. Two **examples of civic participation** include **voting in an election** and **joining a community group** (or volunteering, or contacting an elected official, or running for office) [3].

There are also many ways for Americans to **serve their country**. They can **vote**, **serve in the military**, **serve on a jury**, or **work for the government** [3].

## Paying Taxes

It is **important to pay federal taxes** because the law requires it. Tax money pays for things people share — roads, schools, the military, and many other public services [5]. Federal income taxes are due by **April 15** each year [5].

## Selective Service

All **men ages 18 through 25** living in the United States must **register with the Selective Service** [6]. The reason matters. Registration is **required by law**. It is also a **civic duty**. And it makes the **draft fair** in case the country ever needs to call people to serve again [6].
