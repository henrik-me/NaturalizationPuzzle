The people who wrote the U.S. Constitution had just lived through the rule of a king. They were determined that no one person — and no single group — would ever hold all the power again.

## Why Three Branches?

The Constitution divides the federal government into three branches: the Legislative branch (Congress), the Executive branch (the president), and the Judicial branch (the courts) [1]. The framers used this design so that no branch could become too powerful. This idea is called the **separation of powers** [1].

Each branch can also check the others. Congress writes laws, the president can veto them, and the courts can decide whether laws follow the Constitution. This system is called **checks and balances** [1].

## The Legislative Branch

Congress writes the laws of the United States [2]. It has two parts: the **Senate** and the **House of Representatives** [2].

The Senate has **100** members — two from each of the 50 states [3]. A senator's term is **6 years** [3].

The House has **435** voting members. The number of representatives a state has depends on its population [4]. A representative's term is **2 years** [4].

Because each state has two senators no matter its size, every state has equal power in the Senate. The number of representatives is set by population, so larger states have more.

## The Executive Branch

The **president** leads the executive branch [5]. The president's job includes signing laws, leading the military, and meeting with leaders from other countries [5]. The president serves a term of **4 years** [5].

## The Judicial Branch

The **Supreme Court** is the highest court in the United States [6]. There are **9 seats** on the Court — one Chief Justice and eight associate justices [6]. The Court's job is to decide whether laws and government actions follow the Constitution. This is called **judicial review** [7].
